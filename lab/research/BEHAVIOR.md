## 1. How infants and children learn physics, causes and concepts (was B1)

| Behavior | What children do | How it is measured | When | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Objects persist and move on connected paths | Look longer when an object seems to vanish or jump | Violation-of-expectation (VoE) looking time (Baillargeon, Spelke & Wasserman 1985) | From about 3–5 months | Influential but **contested**: familiarity and perceptual explanations (Rivera, Wakeley & Langer 1999); small samples |
| Core knowledge of objects: cohesion, continuity, contact | Expect things to move only when touched | VoE | Infancy | Well supported as a framework (Spelke & Kinzler 2007) |
| Physical rules learned **separately per kind of event** | Understand "a tall object sticks out of a short occluder" months before the same fact for containers | VoE (Hespos & Baillargeon 2001) | 4.5 months for occlusion, about 7.5 for containment (verified, C03) | Replicated in Baillargeon's lab; a key idea: knowledge has **scope** |
| Surprise drives learning and testing | After seeing an object pass through a wall, infants learn more about *that* object and **test** it: they bang it (solidity); after it hovers, they drop it (support) | Stahl & Feigenson 2015 | 11 months | **Partly replicated** (C03): the preference to explore the surprising object replicated (Perez & Feigenson 2022, Exp. 1), and **showing a hole in the wall that explained the violation abolished it** (Exps. 2–3). The full 2015 package (violation-matched banging vs dropping) has no confirmed direct replication |
| Causal learning from patterns | Pick out which block makes the "blicket detector" go, including backward blocking | Blicket detector (Gopnik & Sobel 2000; Sobel, Tenenbaum & Gopnik 2004) | 3–4 years | Replicated; fits Bayesian causal models (Gopnik et al. 2004) |
| Learning from interventions | Learn more from their own interventions than from watching | Blicket and gear toys (Gopnik 2012 review) | Preschool | Supported |
| Weight and density are one blurred concept at first | Say a big light object and a small heavy one are "the same weight"; mix up "heavy" and "heavy for its size" | Interviews and weighing tasks (Smith, Carey & Wiser 1985) | Differentiated later in childhood | Classic; basis of Carey's conceptual change |
| Conceptual change: inventing a new quantity | Build new concepts (number, weight vs density) not definable in the old ones: "Quinian bootstrapping" | Carey 2009 | Childhood | A theory; strong case studies |

**Why it matters for us.**
- Knowledge with **scope** (event-by-event learning) is how children do it too. Our certificate's "scope" part
  is human-like, not a bureaucratic add-on.
- **Surprise should aim the next experiment at the surprising object, with a test matched to the violation.**
  This is more specific than "explore where error is high".
- **An explained surprise stops being interesting.** Once infants saw a hole that explained the "impossible"
  event, their extra exploration vanished (Perez & Feigenson 2022). The mind's curiosity should likewise
  switch off when a certified claim accounts for the surprise, and stay on while none does.
- **Mass is hard for children for the same reason it was hard for our robot:** throws alone cannot separate
  weight from density (the lab's finding "mass up to a size power"). Children differentiate the two only with
  the right experiences (weighing, sinking). Our mind needs the equivalent: a direct push.

**The ladder the mind should climb, in order** (each rung is a test in section 8):
1. Expects things to persist and move continuously (the nursery).
2. Expects contact causes: pushes move things.
3. Learns each kind of force separately, and knows where each claim holds (scope).
4. When surprised, tests the surprising object with a test matched to the surprise.
5. Finds causes from interventions (which object or force is responsible), including backward blocking.
6. Separates quantities that first look like one (weight vs density): asks for the experiment that separates them.
7. Invents a hidden quantity (mass, charge) that unifies behavior across situations, and certifies it.

## 2. How scientists actually make discoveries (was B2)

| Behavior | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Search two spaces at once**: hypotheses and experiments. Some people theorize first ("theorists"), some experiment first ("experimenters") | Klahr & Dunbar 1988 (SDDS), on a programmable robot toy | Classic; replicated in variants |
| **Unexpected results are common and are the doorway.** First blamed on method; when they repeat, theory changes. Local analogies fix methods; distant analogies explain | Dunbar's in-vivo studies of three molecular-biology labs and one immunology lab (Dunbar 1995, 1997): over half the findings were unexpected (verified, C03) | Observational; widely cited |
| **Magnify the surprise:** when a result is surprising, propose explanations of the surprise and design experiments that isolate it | KEKADA (Kulkarni & Simon 1988) reproduced Krebs's discovery of the **urea cycle** (the ornithine effect), not the citric-acid cycle | A model of one historical case |
| **Invent intrinsic properties** so a law holds across objects: give each object a number (mass, specific heat) | BACON.4 (Bradshaw, Langley & Simon 1980; Langley et al. 1987) | Works when the variables are given; critics note the pre-chosen variables |
| **Model-based reasoning:** build a mechanical analog, abstract its generic structure, run it, carry it over | Maxwell's vortices and idle wheels (Nersessian 2008); Kepler's analogies for the planets' "moving power" (Gentner et al. 1997) | Historical case studies |
| **Confirm early, disconfirm later** | Tweney's analysis of Faraday's diaries (Tweney 1985; the source verified by C03, not the exact wording); people's positive-test strategy (Wason 1960) is often sensible (Klayman & Ha 1987) | Supported |
| **Imagery in thought** | Einstein, in his letter to Hadamard: words play little role; "more or less clear images" do (Hadamard 1945) | Documented in his own words |
| Tesla "built and tested machines in his head" | Tesla's autobiography, *My Inventions* (1919) | **Self-report only**; no independent evidence |

**What separates discovery from routine problem solving:** noticing and pursuing anomalies; choosing experiments
that make rivals disagree; inventing new variables; and using analogy, local for methods and distant for
explanations.

## 3. Curiosity, play and exploration (was B3)

| Behavior | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Confounded evidence makes children explore**, and their play isolates the causes | Schulz & Bonawitz 2007; Cook, Goodman & Schulz 2011 (verified by C02) | Replicated |
| **Surprise relative to beliefs** drives exploration (a surprise is only surprising to someone with a theory) | Bonawitz, van Schijndel, Friel & Schulz 2012 | Supported |
| **Explaining an inconsistency** drives exploration | Legare 2012 | Supported |
| **Curiosity peaks at medium confidence** (inverted U); curiosity improves memory, including for surprising answers | Kang et al. 2009 | Supported |
| Infants look away from sequences too simple **or** too complex ("Goldilocks") | Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin 2012 | Supported |
| **Curiosity = a felt gap in knowledge** | Loewenstein 1994 (information-gap theory) | Influential theory |
| Curiosity follows **learning progress** | Gottlieb & Oudeyer 2018; Oudeyer 2018 (verified) | Theory with growing evidence |
| **Question asking:** children ask many information-seeking questions (about 76 per hour *of conversation with adults*, in transcripts of four children; C03), and with age they ask more efficient, constraint-seeking questions | Chouinard 2007; Ruggeri & Lombrozo 2015 | Supported |
| **Children explore more than adults** and can learn unusual rules adults miss | Lucas et al. 2014; Liquin & Gopnik 2022; Gopnik 2020 (explore early, exploit later) | Supported |
| People **pay for information with no use** | Bennett et al. 2016; FitzGibbon, Lau & Murayama 2020 (a review in *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences*; venue corrected by C03) | Supported: the root of trivia traps |

**For our mind.**
- **Be curious when:**
  - a confident expectation is violated;
  - evidence is confounded, meaning live rivals agree on everything seen;
  - an explanation fails;
  - confidence is medium.
- **Then:** pick the experiment that best separates the live rivals. A child pulls the gears apart; a
  scientist asks constraint-seeking questions. This is J7's expected discrimination.
- **Do not be curious about:**
  - pure noise: the noise model tells it what is unlearnable;
  - trivia: information that bears on no live rival and no open claim.

## 4. Imagination, mental simulation, creativity and insight (was B4)

| Behavior | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Mental rotation** takes time in proportion to the angle | Shepard & Metzler 1971 | Robust |
| **Mental simulation of mechanisms**, step by step along the causal chain | Hegarty 2004 (pulleys, gears) | Supported |
| People judge physical scenes as if running a **noisy physics engine** | Battaglia, Hamrick & Tenenbaum 2013 | Supported for some judgments. **Limits:** people break probability rules in ways a simulator would not (Ludwin-Peery et al. 2020, 2021) |
| **Counterfactual simulation** decides causal judgments: people imagine where the ball would have gone | Gerstenberg et al. 2021 (eye tracking in collision scenes) | Supported. **Directly usable in the ball world** |
| Counterfactuals follow "fault lines": people change what was controllable or exceptional | Byrne 2005 | Robust |
| **Pretend play** is linked to counterfactual reasoning | Buchsbaum et al. 2012; Weisberg & Gopnik 2013 | Correlational. **A review found little evidence that pretend play *causes* better development** (Lillard et al. 2013) |
| **Insight ("aha")** is sudden: no feeling of getting warmer beforehand | Metcalfe & Wiebe 1987 | Robust |
| Insight comes from **changing the representation**: relaxing a constraint, breaking a chunk | Ohlsson 1992; Knoblich et al. 1999 (matchstick arithmetic) | Supported |
| A brain signature precedes insight solutions | Jung-Beeman et al. 2004; Kounios & Beeman 2014 | Supported |
| **Aha solutions are right more often than not, but not always**; false "ahas" happen | Salvi et al. 2016; Danek & Wiley 2017 | Supported: **the aha feeling is a clue, not a proof** |
| **Incubation** (a break) helps, more for divergent tasks | Sio & Ormerod 2009, meta-analysis | Supported, modest |
| **Creativity = novel and appropriate** | Runco & Jaeger 2012 ("the standard definition") | Consensus |
| Measured by **fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration** | Guilford's Alternate Uses Task; the Torrance tests | Standard. **Known flaw:** originality counts rise with fluency (more ideas, more rare ones by chance). Fixes: top-2 scoring (Silvia et al. 2008); expert judges (Amabile 1982) |
| Creativity needs both **divergent** (many ideas) and **convergent** (selecting) thinking | Guilford; Cropley 2006; Campbell 1960 and Simonton: blind variation, selective retention | Supported |

**Your report card, mapped to these constructs, with exact rules fixed in advance:**

| You asked | Construct | Rule for our mind (per problem) |
|---|---|---|
| How many ideas | **Fluency** | Distinct well-formed ideas proposed. Two ideas count as one if they predict the same on a fixed probe set of situations |
| How many approaches | **Flexibility** | Distinct *families* among them: which inputs, which shape, which new term, which hidden quantity |
| How novel | **Originality** | Rarity: −log₂ of how often that idea appears in a reference pool (the baselines' proposals plus its own past proposals on similar problems). Scored on its **top 2** ideas only, to remove the fluency confound |
| How many are right | **Appropriateness** | Fraction of proposed ideas that end up **certified** (D2). Plus the count of ideas that are both certified and original above a set threshold: **novel and right** |
| (a fifth, from Torrance) | **Elaboration** | Scope actually tested for each certified idea |

The five numbers are reported **separately**, never merged into one score, so one cannot be traded for another.

## 5. Understanding others: perspective, belief, intention, feeling, meaning (was B5)

| Behavior | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 1 perspective:** knows *what* another person can see | Flavell et al. 1981 | By about 2–3 years |
| **Level 2 perspective:** knows *how* it looks to them (upside down, far away) | Flavell et al. 1981 | By about 4–5 years |
| **False belief:** predicts that someone looks where they *think* the object is | Wimmer & Perner 1983; meta-analysis Wellman, Cross & Watson 2001 | Explicit tasks: passed from about 4 years, across cultures. Robust |
| "Implicit" false belief in infants | Onishi & Baillargeon 2005 | **Replication problems** (Kulke et al. 2018) |
| Adults compute others' viewpoints fast | Samson et al. 2010 | Contested: may be attention cueing, not mind reading (Heyes 2014) |
| **Understanding intentions:** infants copy an odd action only when it seems chosen | Gergely, Bekkering & Király 2002 (head-touch light box): 69% copied the head touch when the model's hands were free, 21% when they were wrapped (verified, C03) | Interpretation contested |
| **Others' goals and preferences** are inferred by imagining their plans ("inverse planning") | Baker, Saxe & Tenenbaum 2009; Jara-Ettinger et al. 2016 ("naive utility calculus") | Supported, computational |
| **Emotions** are inferred from what happened relative to someone's goals ("affective cognition") | Ong, Zaki & Goodman 2015 | Supported |
| Cognitive empathy (knowing) and affective empathy (sharing) are different | Davis 1983; Shamay-Tsoory et al. 2009 | Supported |
| Readers build **situation models**: time, space, cause, goals, characters | Zwaan & Radvansky 1998 | Robust |
| "Understanding language = embodied simulation" | Glenberg & Kaschak 2002 (action–sentence compatibility) | **The key effect failed a preregistered multi-lab replication** (Morey et al. 2022) |
| **Metaphor:** new metaphors are understood by comparison (structure mapping), familiar ones as categories | Bowdle & Gentner 2005 | Supported |
| **Meaning beyond words:** listeners infer what a speaker meant from what they chose to say | Frank & Goodman 2012 (Rational Speech Act) | Supported |

**"See the flower through your eyes", in behaviors:**
1. Know what you can see from where you stand (Level 1).
2. Know how it looks to you: size, angle, what is hidden (Level 2).
3. Know what you believe after something moved while you were away (false belief).
4. Know what you want, from how you acted (inverse planning).
5. Know how you feel about what happened, from your goals (appraisal).
6. Know what you meant by your words, from what else you could have said (RSA).

**"Understand the author"** = rungs 3–6, applied to a text's writer.

## 6. Learning from others: teaching, feedback, mistakes, confidence (was B6)

| Finding | Evidence | Use for the mind |
|---|---|---|
| **Contingent help:** more help after a failure, less after a success | Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976; Wood & Middleton 1975 | The fading rule (verified in J4 via Salden et al. 2010) |
| **Worked examples help novices and hurt experts** (expertise reversal) | Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003 | Fade by skill, per task family |
| **Self-explanation:** explaining each step to oneself improves learning | Chi et al. 1989, 1994; meta-analysis Bisra et al. 2018: g = 0.55 over 69 effects (verified, C03) | The mind must state *why* an idea fits (a certificate sketch) before it is shown the answer |
| **Comparing two cases** builds a transferable schema; one case rarely transfers | Gick & Holyoak 1980, 1983; Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003 | Present problems in *pairs*; anti-unify the solutions (section 7 of the architecture) |
| Spontaneous far transfer is **rare** | Barnett & Ceci 2002; Sala & Gobet 2017 | Expect zero-shot to be hard; test it only on fresh kinds |
| Experts sort problems by **deep principle**, novices by surface | Chi, Feltovich & Glaser 1981 | A test: does the mind group worlds by law or by look? |
| **Productive failure:** struggling first, then instruction, beats instruction first **for concepts and transfer, not for procedures** | Kapur 2008; meta-analysis Sinha & Kapur 2021: g = 0.36 for conceptual knowledge and transfer, g = −0.03 for procedural (verified, C03); well-designed productive failure does better than the broad "problem first" design | Let it try before teaching when the goal is a new concept (agrees with J4: explore before being taught); drill procedures directly |
| Unsuccessful attempts help later learning | Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009 | Errors in dreams are cheap and useful |
| **Hypercorrection:** confident errors are corrected best after feedback | Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001 | Weight feedback on confident errors most; the lab's "vigilance" |
| Feedback helps on average, but **over a third of feedback interventions made performance worse**, especially feedback aimed at the self rather than the task | Kluger & DeNisi 1996 | Credit about the task, never "praise" |
| **Expected rewards for doing a task can reduce interest in it** (overjustification) | Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973; meta-analysis Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 | The machine analog is v15's finding: paying for success sent 77.9% of rounds to the easiest quest. **Pay for progress (new high-water marks), not for doing** |
| People are overconfident; "Dunning–Kruger" is largely a statistical artifact | Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips 1982; Gignac & Zajenkowski 2020 | Measure calibration directly |
| **Weather forecasters are well calibrated** | Murphy & Winkler 1977 | Calibration comes from frequent, fast, unambiguous feedback in a regular world |
| **Trustworthy intuition needs a regular world and practice with feedback** | Kahneman & Klein 2009 | Our checker supplies the unambiguous feedback; the gut can then be trusted as a guide, never as proof |
| Training in probability reasoning improves forecasting | Mellers et al. 2014 | Proper scores on unsure numbers (D3) |

## 7. Cognitive styles, and a "wide versus narrow" switch (was B7)

The evidence, stated carefully and respectfully:
- **Autism:**
  - Enhanced perceptual functioning: better low-level discrimination in some autistic people (Mottron et
    al. 2006).
  - Weak central coherence was revised to a *bias* toward detail, not a deficit; global processing is intact
    when asked for (Happé & Frith 2006).
  - A meta-analysis found no overall local advantage, but slower global processing (Van der Hallen et al.
    2015).
  - Systemizing (Baron-Cohen 2009) is criticized for self-report measures.
  - Predictive-coding accounts: weaker priors, "hypo-priors" (Pellicano & Burr 2012), or high, inflexible
    precision of prediction errors (Van de Cruys et al. 2014). They are **plausible, with mixed evidence**.
- **ADHD:**
  - Adults with ADHD scored higher on divergent and lower on convergent thinking (White & Shah 2006), with a
    positive follow-up (2011). A 2020 review finds the divergent advantage mainly with high *subclinical* ADHD
    traits, not consistently in diagnosed groups. A 2016 meta-analysis found a pooled association with
    creativity indistinguishable from zero (verified, C03). **Mixed.**
  - Hyperfocus is mostly self-reported (Hupfeld, Abagis & Shah 2019).
- **Dyslexia:** claims of spatial or holistic talent are **not established**. Some positive findings (von
  Károlyi et al. 2003), some negative (Winner et al. 2001).

**What honestly carries over:** not neurotypes, but two **settings of one mind**, each with a cost:

| Setting | What it changes | Good for | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Narrow (detail)** | Weaker priors; more weight on small prediction errors | Faint anomalies: the faint wind, an omitted mechanism | Chases noise |
| **Wide (global)** | Stronger priors; retrieval of distant analogies from the library | Distant unifications; new kinds from old | Misses small details |

A controller switches by **learning progress**: narrow when progress stalls on an anomaly, wide when local
search is exhausted. **It is tested as an engineering choice** (tournament T1), with no claim that it models any
person.

## 8. Behavioral tests for our mind (pass and fail fixed before running)

| # | Test | Setup in our worlds | Pass | Milestone |

| H1 | Persistence and continuity | Nursery: an object hidden by a screen | Predicts reappearance; a "vanished" object raises surprise above the 99th percentile of normal frames | M1 |

## 9. Myths and cautions

- **Einstein's imagery is documented; Tesla's mental test benches are legend** (self-report only).
- **A eureka feeling is not truth.** False insights exist, so our "aha" never replaces the checker.
- **Pretend play has not been shown to cause better thinking** (Lillard et al. 2013). Imagination must be
  *tested*, not assumed useful.
- **"Learning styles" have no support** (Pashler et al. 2008). Don't build "visual" vs "verbal" modules on
  that basis.
- **The 10,000-hour rule overstates practice:** deliberate practice explained 12% of performance differences
  overall (Macnamara et al. 2014).
- **Far transfer from brain training, chess or music is near zero** (Sala & Gobet 2017).
- **Dunning–Kruger is mostly a statistical artifact** (Gignac & Zajenkowski 2020).
- **Embodied-language effects:** the action–sentence effect failed a multi-lab replication (Morey et al. 2022).
- **Infant "implicit theory of mind":** replication failures (Kulke et al. 2018).
- **Copying people where it hurts our goals:** people confirm early and chase trivia, and their confidence
  is poorly calibrated without feedback. Our mind should keep the good parts (curiosity at surprises,
  comparison, contingent teaching) and use the checker against the bad ones.
